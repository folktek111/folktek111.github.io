Abstract

You wibble your way back to wobbly.

Level 1 · 1687

Newton's Laws of Motion

In 1687 Newton wrote down three laws of motion. Any cat could have shown him in four seconds with a glass of water and the edge of a table. Read the laws closely and all three describe a seesaw.

1st law: ΣF = 0 ⇒ v = constant 2nd law: F = m·a = m·ẍ 3rd law: FAB = −FBA Take anything sitting in balance at x₀: F(x₀) = 0 Nudge it by a tiny amount δ: F(x₀ + δ) ≈ F(x₀) + F′(x₀)·δ = F′(x₀)·δ If it's stable, it pushes back, so F′(x₀) < 0. Call it −k: m·δ̈ = −k·δ That is the equation of an oscillator.
∴ Everything is wobbly.Anything stable wobbles when nudged. Anything unstable falls over (see Level 8, or the glass).
Level 2 · 1678

Hooke's Law & Simple Harmonic Motion

Robert Hooke first published his law as an anagram, ceiiinosssttuv (for real), and only revealed it two years later: ut tensio, sic vis ("as the stretch, so the force"). He was so worried someone would pinch the idea, he hid it in a word puzzle. The theory was wobbly from day one.

F = −k·x m·ẍ + k·x = 0 x(t) = A·cos(ωt + φ), ω = √(k/m) Energy: E = ½m·ẋ² + ½k·x² = ½k·A² (constant) It never stops. It sloshes between motion and stretch forever. Add friction: ẍ + 2γẋ + ω₀²x = 0 x(t) = A·e−γt·cos(ωdt + φ) Even when it calms down, it gets to stillness by wobbling.

Chemical bonds behave like little springs, so everything made of atoms has springs in it.

∴ Everything is wobbly.Springs go boing. It took two years to publish.
Level 3 · 1827 / 1905

Brownian Motion

In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown watched tiny particles from pollen floating in water under his microscope. They never stopped jiggling. In 1905 Einstein explained why: they're being bumped from every side by water molecules, which are wobbling because they're warm.

How far a jiggling particle wanders (in one direction): ⟨x²⟩ = 2·D·t Stokes–Einstein relation: D = kB·T / (6π·η·r) Worked example: a 1 µm grain in water at 20 °C (r = 0.5×10⁻⁶ m, η = 1.0×10⁻³ Pa·s, T = 293 K) D = (1.381×10⁻²³ × 293) / (6π × 10⁻³ × 0.5×10⁻⁶) = 4.3×10⁻¹³ m²/s Distance wandered in one second: √(2·D·t) ≈ 0.9 µm about its own width, every second, forever

Jean Perrin confirmed Einstein's numbers by experiment in 1908, which finally convinced the doubters that atoms were real. He got the Nobel Prize for it in 1926.

∴ Everything is wobbly.Anything warmer than absolute zero is being jostled. (Level 6 says even absolute zero doesn't help.)
Level 4 · 1747 / 1924

Waves & the de Broglie Relation

Chain a lot of wobbly springs together and the wobble starts travelling. That's a wave. Then Louis de Broglie pointed out that matter itself does this.

A chain of masses joined by springs: m·ẍₙ = k·(xₙ₊₁ − 2xₙ + xₙ₋₁) Shrink the spacing to zero: ∂²u/∂t² = c²·∂²u/∂x² (the wave equation) u(x,t) = A·sin(kx − ωt) de Broglie: λ = h / p Worked example: one 4 kg cat strolling at 1 m/s λ = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s / (4 kg × 1 m/s) = 1.66×10⁻³⁴ m

Confirmed for electrons by Davisson and Germer in 1927: fire them at a crystal and they diffract like light. The cat has not been tested yet.

∴ Everything is wobbly.Including the cat. Very, very slightly.
Level 5 · 1927

Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle WOBBLY

You can't build a wave that is in one exact place and also has one exact wavelength. Since wavelength is momentum (Level 4), nothing can be both perfectly located and perfectly still. Physicists call this a profound limit on human knowledge.

Cats call it Tuesday.

A pulse squeezed into a small space needs a spread of wavelengths (Fourier analysis): Δx · Δk ≥ ½ Momentum is wavenumber times ℏ: p = ℏk ⇒ Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2 Worked example: an electron held inside an atom (Δx ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m) Δp ≥ 1.055×10⁻³⁴ / (2 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 5.3×10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s Δv = Δp / mₑ ≈ 5.3×10⁻²⁵ / 9.11×10⁻³¹ ≈ 580 km/s just from being contained
∴ Everything is wobbly.Wobbly isn't a mood. It's a lower bound.
Level 6 · 1926–1948

Zero-Point Energy

Take the spring from Level 2, make it quantum, and cool it to absolute zero. Does it stop? No. Stopping would mean an exact position and an exact momentum, which Level 5 forbids. Erwin Schrödinger, yes, the f#¢%¡ñ9 cat-in-a-box guy*. Well, he worked out exactly how much it keeps jiggling.

*He could have used another animal.

E = p²/2m + ½mω²x² Use Level 5, Δp ≥ ℏ/(2Δx): E ≥ ℏ²/(8m·Δx²) + ½mω²·Δx² Find the lowest possible energy: dE/dΔx = 0 ⇒ Δx² = ℏ/(2mω) Emin = ℏω/4 + ℏω/4 = ℏω/2 Exact answer (Schrödinger): Eₙ = ℏω(n + ½), n = 0, 1, 2, … At n = 0 there is still ½ℏω left. Always.

Evidence: helium stays liquid at normal pressure even close to absolute zero, because its zero-point jiggle is too strong for it to freeze. The Casimir force, where two metal plates in a vacuum get pulled together, was measured by Lamoreaux in 1997.

∴ Everything is wobbly.Even doing nothing. Absolute stillness isn't on the menu in this universe.
Level 7 · 1963

Chaos Theory & the Butterfly Effect WONKY

Edward Lorenz was running a simple weather model on a computer. He restarted a run with a number rounded from 0.506127 to 0.506 and got completely different weather. A tiny wobble, fed back through the system, grew until nothing could be predicted. He called it the butterfly effect. He could just as easily have called it the cat-walking-across-the-keyboard effect.

The Lorenz system: ẋ = σ(y − x) ẏ = x(ρ − z) − y ż = xy − βz with σ = 10, ρ = 28, β = 8/3 Two starting points a tiny distance δ₀ apart drift apart as: |δ(t)| ≈ |δ₀| · eλt, λ ≈ 0.91 (Lyapunov exponent) Error of one millionth reaches size 1 after: t = ln(10⁶) / 0.91 ≈ 15 time units The logistic map, the simplest road to chaos: xₙ₊₁ = r·xₙ·(1 − xₙ) r < 3 → settles down r = 3 → starts wobbling between two values r ≈ 3.57 → chaos
∴ Wonky time.Feed a tiny wobble back into itself and it grows until nothing makes sense. Wonky, with a butterfly on it.
Level 8 · 1890s–1940s

Unstable Spirals WONKY

Wobble plus positive feedback. Each time round the loop it gains a little instead of losing a little. Example: a cat chasing its own tail.

Near any state, a system behaves like: ẋ = A·x The eigenvalues of A come out as: λ = α ± iω In polar form: r(t) = r₀ · eαt θ(t) = θ₀ + ωt α < 0 → stable spiral (wobble settles home) α = 0 → centre (pure wobble, forever) α > 0 → unstable spiral (WONKY) Barkhausen criterion for feedback howl: |loop gain| ≥ 1 and loop phase = 0 (mod 2π) Hopf bifurcation: the moment α crosses zero, a calm wobble turns into a runaway one.
∴ Wonky time.Wonky isn't a lack of wobble. It's wobble that's started feeding itself.
Level 9 · 1983

Simulated Annealing WIBBLE

Blacksmiths have always known it: heat the metal, then cool it slowly, and the atoms settle into a strong, even crystal. In 1983 Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi turned this into maths for getting any system out of a rut. Shake it up enough to climb out of the dip, then calm down gently.

Metropolis rule: accept a move that makes things worse with probability P = e−ΔE / T Worked example: stuck in a dip with a wall of height ΔE = 1 T = 1 → P = e−1 ≈ 0.37 wibbly enough to climb out T = 0.1 → P = e−10 ≈ 0.000045 frozen in the rut Cool slowly enough, Tk ∝ 1 / ln(k) and you are guaranteed to find the deepest, steadiest state. Cool too fast and you freeze into the first rut you find.
∴ The way of the wibble.Shake it up to get out of the rut. Then cool down gently. Get serious too early and you freeze the wonk in place.
Level 10 · 1665 / 1975

Synchronisation WIBBLE

In 1665 Christiaan Huygens, ill in bed, noticed two pendulum clocks hanging from the same beam had fallen into step all by themselves. Fireflies, metronomes on a shared board and heart pacemaker cells all do the same. Nobody conducts. Loose wobblers nudge each other until they find a shared rhythm.

The Kuramoto model (1975): θ̇ᵢ = ωᵢ + (K/N) · Σⱼ sin(θⱼ − θᵢ) How in step everyone is: r·eiψ = (1/N) · Σⱼ eiθⱼ r = 0 → everyone wobbling alone r = 1 → everyone in step Below a critical coupling Kc, nothing happens. Above it (natural tempos spread with half-width γ, Kc = 2γ): r = √(1 − Kc/K) Worked example: K = 2·Kc ⇒ r = √½ ≈ 0.71
∴ The way of the wibble.Coherence isn't forced on the wobble. It comes out of it.
Level 11 · 1948

Feynman's Path Integral WIBBLE

How does a particle get from A to B? Feynman's answer: it tries every possible path at once, much like a cat getting from the sofa to the food bowl, including the route across your face. The wild paths cancel each other out, and the sensible path survives because all its neighbours agree with it. That's the way back from wonky: not one correct move, but enough variation that the coherent route is what's left.

⟨xf | xi⟩ = ∫ 𝒟x(t) · eiS[x]/ℏ S[x] = ∫ ( ½m·ẋ² − V(x) ) dt Far from the classical path, S changes fast, so the phases spin round and the paths cancel. Near it, δS = 0, so the phases line up and add together. δS = 0 ⇒ m·ẍ = −∂V/∂x That's Newton's second law. We're back at Level 1. In Level 8 terms, wibbling is whatever brings α back below zero: r(t) = r₀ · e−|α|t (the spiral turns inward)
∴ The way of the wibble.The way out of wonky is to wibble. Order is whatever survives the wobbling.
Level 12 · 1915 / 2015

General Relativity & Gravitational Waves

Spacetime wobbles too. Simplify Einstein's field equations for small ripples and you get the wave equation from Level 4. LIGO picked one up in 2015. It took a century, two colliding black holes and a pair of 4 km lasers to measure a wobble smaller than a proton.

Einstein field equations: Gμν + Λgμν = (8πG/c⁴) Tμν Weak field: flat spacetime plus a small ripple gμν = ημν + hμν, |h| ≪ 1 Lorenz gauge, empty space: □ h̄μν = 0 ( −(1/c²)·∂²/∂t² + ∇² ) h̄μν = 0 → the wave equation again, travelling at the speed of light. GW150914 (14 Sept 2015): two black holes, roughly 36 and 29 solar masses, about 1.3 billion light-years away. Peak strain: h ≈ 10⁻²¹ ΔL = h · L ≈ 10⁻²¹ × 4 km = 4×10⁻¹⁸ m A few thousandths of the width of a proton.

Turned into sound, the signal was a chirp rising from about 35 to 250 Hz. A cat's purr sits at roughly 25 to 150 Hz. We draw no conclusions. We just think it's interesting.

∴ Everything is wobbly.Including the stage everything happens on.
